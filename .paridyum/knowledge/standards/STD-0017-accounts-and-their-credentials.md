# STD-0017 — Accounts and their credentials

## Status

**`accepted`, and it should stay that way.** Every rule below is judgement — whether a name is
ambiguous, whether two people are sharing a login — and ADR-0002 is explicit that a check
approximating judgement with a wordlist is worse than no check, because it looks like
enforcement. C-0007's abbreviation section made the same call for the same reason.

## What this covers, and what it does not

**Covers:** an account the estate creates in order to operate something — a Unix account on a
host, a login in a service's own admin UI, a role in a database, a bot identity in a
third-party tool.

**Does not cover** the product's identity model. `operator`, `tenant` and `account` are defined
terms with columns behind them (GLO-0001, GLO-0002, GLO-0003), and nothing here touches them.
That separation is the first rule's whole point.

## Why this exists at all

C-0007 names repositories, crates, HTTP APIs, databases, events, environment variables, images
and Helm releases. It does not name accounts, and the gap is not an oversight of drafting — it
is simply a namespace nobody had needed yet.

It came up the first time a service asked *"choose a username"* and all four obvious answers
were wrong: one collided with a Unix account on the same host, one collided with an unprivileged
service account whose whole purpose is to hold no privilege, one collided with a glossary term
that names a paying customer, and one was `admin`.

## The rules

> **1 · An account is named for the role it plays, never for the person holding it.**

A person's name on a shared-purpose account is wrong twice: it is wrong the day they leave, and
it is wrong every day before that, because it implies an attributability the account does not
have.

**The exception is an account that genuinely IS a person** — the operator's own Unix login,
their GitHub identity. Those keep the person's name, because there the name is the truth. C-0003
depends on it: an operator who can reach a host is an operator whose commits are attributable.

> **2 · A name must be unambiguous in every namespace it will appear in, not just the one it is
> created in.**

The failure is never at creation. It is six months later, in an incident, when *"the sunshine
password"* and *"rohan's password"* are two different secrets with one name and somebody tries
the wrong one on the third attempt of a lockout.

**Four names are reserved and must not be taken:**

| Reserved | Why |
| --- | --- |
| any existing **Unix account on the same host** | two credentials, one name — the failure above |
| `paridyum` | RFC-0035 D2's **unprivileged** service account. Reusing it for anything privileged inverts a control while leaving its name intact, which is the silent kind of wrong |
| `operator`, `tenant`, `account` | GLO-0001, GLO-0002, GLO-0003. These name parties in the product's tenancy model, and GLO-0004 records that nested tenancy is the single easiest thing here to get structurally wrong |
| `admin`, `root`, `user` | say nothing about what is administered, and are the first three strings any credential-stuffing list tries |

> **3 · The grammar is `<service>-<role>`, lowercase, hyphenated.**

`sunshine-admin`. `grafana-viewer`. The service qualifier is what makes rule 2 hold by
construction rather than by checking, and it costs nothing: the name is typed twice in its life.

Where the service *is* the host — a Unix account — the service qualifier is redundant and the
role alone is the name, as `paridyum` already is.

> **4 · One person, one account. A login two people share is not an account, it is a secret.**

When a second person needs access, create a second account. **If the service cannot represent
one — and many small self-hosted services cannot — that is a gap to write down where the
service is documented, not a reason to hand the password over.** A shared login makes every
subsequent audit question unanswerable, so the estate should at least know which of its services
have made that unanswerable.

> **5 · The credential is generated, never chosen, and the username travels with it.**

Generated by the password manager, unique, and never typed from memory — which is what makes
length free. **Generate alphanumeric.** Not for entropy: a 40-character alphanumeric password is
far past any threshold that matters. It is because the reset path for these services is
routinely a shell command, and a password containing `$`, `!` or a quote turns a recovery
procedure into a quoting bug at the exact moment somebody is already having a bad day.

The username goes in the vault item's Username field, per RUN-0004's item convention. A password
whose username is a guess is half a credential.

## Where the register is, and why it is not in git

**The password-manager folder is the register.** RUN-0004's convention makes items enumerable by
folder and gives each a `governed_by` field pointing at the artifact that justifies it. That is
already a list of every account the estate holds a credential for.

A second list, committed to a repository, was considered and rejected. It would drift from the
vault the first time somebody rotated something in a hurry, and a stale account register is
worse than none — it answers *"who has access?"* confidently and wrongly. It would also be a
committed inventory of every account name on every host, which is a shopping list for anyone who
later gets read access to the repository.

**What does belong in git** is the *decision* — that a service has an admin account at all, what
it is for, and any rule-4 gap it carries. That is a sentence in the artifact governing the
service, not a row in a table of names.
